How to Sell a House in Probate in Los Angeles

A plain-English guide for executors: the two ways a probate home sale works in California, how long it takes, and what to do with the house while you wait.

Farhad Yasharpour, REALTOR® · Keller Williams Realty
November 18, 2026 · About 8 minutes to read

Quick answer

In California, you can usually sell a house during probate once the court appoints you and issues your Letters. How you sell depends on the authority the court gives you. With full authority, the sale works much like a normal sale: you give the heirs a Notice of Proposed Action, wait at least 15 days, and close if no one objects. With limited authority, the sale needs court confirmation: the offer must be at least 90% of the probate referee's appraised value, and other buyers can outbid it at the hearing. Your probate attorney will tell you which path your case is on.

1. First, you need Letters

Before anyone can sell a house that is going through probate, the court has to appoint a personal representative. If there is a will, that person is usually called the executor. If there is no will, the court appoints an administrator. Either way, the court issues a document called Letters (Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration). Your Letters are your proof that you have the legal power to act for the estate.

Until Letters are issued, no one should sign a listing agreement or accept an offer on the house. Your attorney files the petition and handles the hearing. My part usually starts here: understanding the house, its condition and its value, so you have a plan ready the moment you are appointed.

2. Full or limited authority: the question that decides everything

Most California estates are handled under a law called the Independent Administration of Estates Act. It lets the personal representative manage the estate without going back to court for every decision. When the court appoints you, it gives you one of two levels of authority, and that choice decides how the house will be sold.

Full authority

You can usually sell the house without a court hearing. You give the heirs and beneficiaries advance notice, and if no one objects, the sale goes forward.

Limited authority

You can handle most estate business, but a sale of the house has to be confirmed by the court at a hearing, where other buyers can bid.

Your Letters say which authority you have. If you are not sure, ask your attorney. It is the first thing I ask about too, because it changes how the listing, the offers and the timeline should be planned.

3. Path 1: Selling with full authority

With full authority, the sale looks a lot like any other home sale. The home is listed, buyers make offers, and you negotiate the price and terms. The main difference is one extra step before closing:

  1. Accept an offer. The purchase agreement is signed by you as personal representative.
  2. Send a Notice of Proposed Action. Your attorney usually prepares it. It goes to the heirs, beneficiaries and anyone else entitled to notice, and describes the sale and its terms.
  3. Wait at least 15 days. The notice has to be mailed or delivered at least 15 days before the sale can go forward. This gives everyone time to object.
  4. Close. If no one objects, escrow can close without a court hearing.

If someone does object, the sale can still happen, but it would need the court's approval. That is one reason it helps to keep the whole family informed from the start, so the notice holds no surprises.

Good to know: even with full authority, a personal representative can choose to ask the court to confirm the sale. Your attorney can tell you whether that makes sense in your situation.

4. Path 2: Selling with court confirmation

With limited authority, the court has to confirm the sale. It takes longer, and the buyer you accept is not guaranteed to be the buyer who closes. Here is how it usually works:

  1. The house is appraised by the probate referee. A probate referee is a court-appointed appraiser. For a private sale to be confirmed, the appraisal has to be within one year of the hearing.
  2. You accept an offer of at least 90% of that value. For example, if the referee values the house at $900,000, the court will not confirm an offer below $810,000.
  3. Your attorney asks the court to confirm the sale, and a hearing date is set.
  4. At the hearing, other buyers can overbid. Anyone who wants the house can come to court and bid more. If they do, it becomes a small auction in the courtroom.
  5. The court confirms the sale to the highest qualified bidder, and escrow closes.

How the overbid works

California sets the size of the first overbid by formula: it has to beat the accepted offer by at least 10% of the first $10,000 plus 5% of the rest. After that, the judge sets the bidding steps. On a typical Los Angeles price, that first jump is large:

Accepted offer$900,000
10% of the first $10,000+ $1,000
5% of the remaining $890,000+ $44,500
Minimum first overbid$945,500

For the estate, overbidding can be a good thing, because it can raise the price. For buyers, it is a risk, and some buyers prefer to skip court-confirmation sales. Planning the listing around this, from the price to how offers are presented, is part of a good probate sale.

5. How long it takes

Probate itself often takes many months. California gives creditors at least four months after Letters are issued to file claims, so even simple estates rarely close quickly, and many take a year or more.

The good news is that you usually don't have to wait for probate to finish to sell the house. Once you have Letters, the sale can often happen while the rest of the estate is still being settled. Roughly:

  • Full authority: about the time of a normal sale, plus the 15-day notice period.
  • Court confirmation: add the time to get a hearing date on the court's calendar, which can be several weeks or more.

Your attorney can give you a realistic timeline for your case. Knowing it early helps you plan for the house's costs while you wait.

6. What to do with the house while you wait

Between the funeral and the sale, the house often sits empty. That is when problems start. A few things protect the estate's biggest asset:

  • Secure it. Change or re-key the locks, keep track of who has keys, and store valuables and important papers safely.
  • Check the insurance. Many homeowner's policies change when a home is vacant. Call the insurance company and ask what coverage the house has now.
  • Keep the utilities on, so the house can be shown, inspected and kept from damage.
  • Keep it looking lived-in. Lawn care, mail pickup and regular check-ins make a house less of a target.
  • Keep paying the bills, such as the mortgage, property taxes and insurance. Ask your attorney how the estate should handle them.

If you live out of the area, this is where a local agent can help most: meeting vendors at the house, sending photos and videos, and keeping an eye on things so you don't have to fly in.

7. Sell as-is, or prepare it first?

Many probate homes sell as-is, and that is often the simplest path. But a light clean-out, fresh paint or a few small repairs can sometimes add more to the price than they cost. Other times they don't, and the money is better left in the estate.

The honest answer depends on the house. Before you spend anything, compare two numbers side by side: what the home could sell for as it is today, and what it could sell for if it were prepared, minus the cost of preparing it. Those numbers also make family conversations much easier, because everyone can see the same facts.

8. Common questions

Do I have to wait until probate is finished to sell the house?

Usually not. Once the court appoints you and issues your Letters, the house can often be sold while probate is still open. How it is sold depends on the authority the court gave you.

Does a probate house sale always need a court hearing?

No. With full authority, the sale can usually go forward after a Notice of Proposed Action if no one objects. With limited authority, the sale needs court confirmation at a hearing.

Can a family member stop the sale?

With full authority, an heir or beneficiary who receives the Notice of Proposed Action can object, which would send the sale to the court for approval. Talking openly with the family early is the best way to avoid surprises.

What is an overbid?

In a court-confirmed sale, other buyers can bid at the hearing. The first overbid must beat the accepted offer by at least 10% of the first $10,000 plus 5% of the rest. After that, the judge sets the bidding steps.

Do I need to clean out or fix up the house before selling?

No. Many probate homes sell as-is. Sometimes a light clean-out or a few small fixes are worth it, and sometimes they are not. Compare the numbers both ways before spending estate money.
